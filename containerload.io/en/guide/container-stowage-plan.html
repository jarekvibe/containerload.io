How to create a container stowage plan

A stowage plan shows which package sits where in the container — a drawing plus a list. It answers three questions at once: does it all fit, how is it loaded and what should the consignee expect. With the 3D calculator it comes out of your cargo dimensions, without Excel and without a drawing program.

What belongs in a stowage plan

There is no standard for it — but there is a consensus about what it has to deliver. A usable stowage plan states:

  • The container with type and internal dimensions (20′ GP, 40′ HC …), so the drawing has a scale.
  • Every position with description, quantity, dimensions and weight — that is the packing list at the same time.
  • The position in the container, at least as a picture, ideally with tiers and rows.
  • The totals: volume, weight, loading metres used and utilisation against volume and payload.
  • What is left over: what did not fit and how many containers it takes in total.

What a geometric stowage plan is not: a load securing calculation. Lashings, friction coefficients, centre of gravity and axle loads are not in it and have to be checked separately. Mixing the two sells a drawing as a safety certificate.

Stackability is where most stowage plans run out of air

A plan that treats every package as freely stackable looks good and is worthless at the loading dock. So each position carries what it actually takes:

  • Not stackable — stands on the floor, nothing underneath it.
  • Limited stacking — "one on top" means one further tier, no more.
  • Stackable up to … cm — the way it is written in enquiries. The tower may only reach that height above the container floor.
  • Rotatable or not — goods that may stand crosswise often add several floor positions.

The calculator takes these per position and sticks to them — even when less fits as a result. A longer package is not placed on a shorter one so that it ends in mid-air at the edge: what sits on top has to stand on something.

When it turns into more than one container

That is not an error, it is a result. The calculator then says how many containers it takes, distributes the cargo across them and reports volume and weight per container — each one is placed, weighed and hauled separately, after all. The weight is levelled as evenly as possible without needing one container more than necessary.

Frequent questions

What is a stowage plan?
A representation of which packages are loaded into a container in which arrangement — a drawing plus a position list with dimensions, weights and totals.

Who prepares the stowage plan?
Usually the shipper or the forwarder who packs. For groupage it is the container packing station; for FCL shipments often the shipper themselves.

Is a stowage plan mandatory?
Not generally — the VGM declaration is. In practice consignees, customs and insurers ask for one regularly, because it documents what was in the container and how it stood.

How to load a truck: five steps to a load plan

Loading a truck comes down to four things, in this order: space (do the goods fit, how many loading metres), weight (payload and axle load), sequence (what comes off first goes at the rear) and securing (no gaps, nothing slides or tips). Plan them beforehand and there are no surprises at the dock – the free 3D calculator shows all four in one picture.

1. Pick the right vehicle

First the question whether the goods fit at all – by floor, height and weight. The common vehicles with their typical internal dimensions:

How many pallets of which type fit exactly, the pallet calculator works out for every vehicle at once; the billing figure is in the loading metre calculator.

2. Spread the weight: axle loads

A semi-trailer rests on the tractor at the front (on the kingpin) and on its axle group at the rear. If heavy goods stand only at the headboard, too much weight presses on the tractor. If they stand only at the rear, the axle group is overloaded and the drive axle lacks load up front. Typical limits: around 11 t fifth-wheel load and 24 t for the tri-axle group (German StVZO § 34).

Example: 18 euro pallets at 1,000 kg each, not stackable. Placed straight against the headboard they take up 7.2 m – and around 15.4 t rest on the kingpin. With a 2.1 m gap to the headboard the fifth-wheel load drops to 11.0 t and the axle group carries 14.0 t.

That gap then has to be secured: the load no longer has form-fit at the front. The 3D calculator works out this guide value for the curtainsider. If the fifth-wheel load is too high, it first spreads the load flatter across the floor and then moves it back only as far as needed. What is binding is the vehicle’s load distribution plan: it shows how much load is allowed at which point of the floor.

On top comes the gross weight: in Germany and most of the EU an articulated truck may weigh 40 t, 44 t in combined transport and in the UK – more on the weight limits on the road.

3. Sequence: first off means last on

If the route has several drops, load in reverse: the goods for the last drop first, against the headboard; those for the first drop last, at the rear. That way nothing has to be reshuffled at any dock. In the 3D calculator every item gets a stop number (under “show item numbers & stop fields”). Stop 1 then sits at the rear, and goods for different stops are not mixed – that can cost space, but it saves the reshuffling.

4. Stack – but only what can carry it

Stacking saves loading metres: 26 euro pallets two high take 5.2 instead of 10.4 ldm. It costs height and raises the centre of gravity, though. Heavy at the bottom, light on top; stack only what can carry the load above it; and the internal height of around 2.70 m on a curtainsider applies to the whole stack. In the calculator you set for each item whether it is stackable, how often and up to which height.

5. Secure: no gaps, nothing slides

A curtainsider’s curtain does not hold any load. Loads are secured by form-fit – goods tight against the headboard and each other, gaps filled with empty pallets or dunnage – or by friction with lashing straps and anti-slip mats, usually both. The load must be stowed and secured so that it does not slide, tip over, roll about or fall off even under full braking or a sudden evasive manoeuvre. Rules differ by country: in the EU the European best practice guidelines on cargo securing for road transport describe the state of the art; in Germany drivers, loaders and vehicle keepers share the responsibility (§ 22 StVO) and the VDI 2700 guideline series sets the technical rules.

The securing check in the 3D calculator shows gaps in the calculated stowage against the CTU Code guide values – single gaps up to 5 cm, total per direction under 15 cm – and marks them in the picture. Which securing equipment is needed is decided at the loading point; the check does not replace an assessment by a cargo-securing standard.

Frequent questions

How do you load a truck correctly?
In four steps: pick a vehicle by floor, height and weight, spread the weight so that the fifth-wheel load and the axle group stay within limits, stow by drop (first drop at the rear) and secure the load without gaps and against sliding.

Where does heavy cargo go on a truck?
At the bottom and spread across the floor so that neither the tractor nor the axle group is overloaded – not all against the headboard and not all at the rear. The vehicle’s load distribution plan is binding.

How many pallets fit on a truck?
A curtainsider takes 34 euro pallets single-tier, a swap body 18, a 7.5-t truck 12 and a van 6 – more when stacked, as long as height and payload allow.

Who is responsible for load securing?
Not only the driver: in Germany loaders and vehicle keepers share the responsibility (§ 22 StVO). The load must not slide, tip over or fall off even under full braking or a sudden evasive manoeuvre.
